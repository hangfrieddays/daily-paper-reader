Abstract 

Recent Text-to-Image (T2I) models achieve remarkable visual image generation performance, but they can still generate NSFW (Not-Safe-For-Work) contents, in-cluding violent or explicit images. Existing safety checker mechanisms are largely confined to pre-generation filtering (e.g. prompt-level text classifiers) or post-hoc moderation applied after an image is completely synthesized. However, adversar-ial attack methods operate over a much broader space. This imbalance highlights the need for a safety mechanism that intervenes during the generation process. We propose an in-generation safety framework that monitors the denoising tra-jectory and detects emerging NSFW signals from intermediate representations. Rather than merely detecting NSFW generations, our method applies reinforce-ment learning to generate safe images from NSFW prompts. By coupling in-generation detection with controllable steering, our approach mitigates unsafe tra-jectories even when NSFW signals emerge after generation has already begun. Experiments results show that our method consistently outperforms existing safe image generation methods across both standard and adversarial evaluation sets, while preserving perceptual quality and prompt fidelity. Code will be released upon acceptance.

1 Introduction 

Text-to-Image (T2I) models [9, 37] have achieved remarkable progress in generating high-resolution images. Despite these advances, current T2I models still exhibit critical problems regarding safety: they can generate NSFW (Not-Safe-For-Work) contents [35], including sexual and violent images as well as copyrighted or otherwise restricted materials. To mitigate these problems, a lot of research have proposed safety checkers intended to prevent models from generating NSFW contents. However, most existing safety checker architectures remain stage-limited and passive. The most common architecture is to block generation before the denoising process begins by applying text-based filtering at the prompt level [12, 27, 30, 45], or to reject final results after generation completes by classifying the final image in pixel space [23, 35, 39]. While such approaches are practical and widely deployed, they provide only passive protection. Therefore, they are vulnerable to adversarial attacks to generate NSFW contents, which can be applied throughout the whole T2I process. Adversarial attacks against safety checkers are increasingly multi-faceted and operate across multi-ple stages. Attack methods include prompt-based manipulations that evade text filters [20, 28, 44], attacks targeting the guidance mechanisms, and latent-space attacks that directly perturb the de-noising trajectory [26, 35, 36]. Recent efforts have started to broaden the range of safety checkers beyond the prompt and final image. For example, Safe Latent Diffusion (SLD) [38] modifies intermediate representations by projecting latent states toward a “safe” subspace. Nevertheless, such approaches remain largely reactive and brittle because the checker operates as a one-shot safeguard, so they are vulnerable to adversarial perturbations injected after the safety applied. Motivated by this gap, we propose the first framework that introduces an active reinforcement learn-ing (RL) based safety checker agent that intervenes during the denoising trajectory of T2I models (Figure 1). We treat the denoising process as an RL trajectory and formulate safety enforcement as a sequential decision-making problem. At each denoising timestep, an RL agent observes the current generation state and predicts whether the ongoing trajectory is likely to produce an NSFW output. When the agent detects high NSFW risk, it intervenes online to steer the trajectory toward safe generation by guiding the noise predicted, effectively transforming the unsafe path into a safe one rather than merely blocking or rejecting the output. This active, step-wise intervention provides three key advantages. First, even if adversarial attacks emerge after generation has already begun, the agent can still detect NSFW signals during gen-eration and correct the trajectory before the final image is produced, providing robustness against multi-stage adversarial attacks [21, 44]. Second, unlike existing safe image generation methods that require retraining or fine-tuning the T2I model, our framework keeps the pretrained T2I model fully frozen and trains only a lightweight intervention policy, enabling plug-and-play deployment across diverse T2I architectures including both diffusion models [37] and flow matching models [9]. Third, by incorporating a terminal aesthetic reward into the policy training objective, our method explicitly preserves perceptual quality alongside safety enforcement, avoiding the quality degradation com-monly observed in existing approaches. In short, our method expands safety enforcement from a passive, stage specific checker into an adaptive, trajectory-aware control mechanism that is robust to attacks occurring at any point in the T2I generation process. 

2 Related Work 

Text-to-Image (T2I) Models. T2I models synthesize images conditioned on text prompts through an iterative sampling trajectory that progressively transforms a random latent variable into a clean image. This family includes both diffusion based models [16], which learn to reverse a predefined noising process, and flow matching based models [25], which learn a continuous vector field trans-porting samples from noise to data distribution. To reduce computational cost, practical T2I models operate in a compressed latent space via a variational autoencoder (VAE) [37], decoding the final latent into pixel space after sampling. Prominent systems such as the Stable Diffusion family [9, 37] combine pretrained text encoders with iterative generation models for textual conditioning. De-spite their remarkable image quality and prompt alignment, these models continue to exhibit critical safety risks, particularly the generation of NSFW content. 2NSFW Detection and Safety Checker. Safety checker research for T2I models broadly divides into two approaches. The first applies prompt-level filtering during the text embedding step, block-ing generation when a prompt is detected as NSFW via similarity against the predefined NSFW words [12, 27, 30, 45]. The second classifies the final generated image after the full denoising trajectory completes, measuring similarity against NSFW reference images [23, 35, 39]. Both ap-proaches passively detect NSFW content at a single fixed stage– before or after generation– leaving the intermediate denoising trajectory entirely unconstrained and thus highly vulnerable to adversar-ial attacks. 

Safe Image Generation. To move beyond filtering, recent work on safe image generation has shifted toward converting NSFW content into safe outputs. A class of methods operates at the prompt or embedding level, detecting and purifying unsafe token embeddings by projecting them away from toxic concept subspaces using CLIP-based similarity [3, 33]. Another class intervenes directly in the denoising trajectory, steering latent updates away from unsafe directions by condi-tioning on unsafe concept prompts at each diffusion step [38]. However, all of the above methods share a common limitation: they intervene at a single fixed stage of the pipeline. Since adversarial attacks can target every stage of the generation process– including the prompt, the denoising trajec-tory, and the final generated image– a one-stage correction leaves the system inherently vulnerable to attacks that operate at any other stage. 

Adversarial Attack. Adversarial attacks against T2I safety checkers fall into two categories. Input-level attacks modify unsafe prompts to preserve NSFW semantics while reducing similarity to predefined NSFW keywords, evading prompt-based filters [20, 26, 28, 46]. Output-level attacks instead manipulate the visual representation of the generated image so that unsafe semantics remain perceptually present while the image embedding shifts away from the checker’s reference concepts [6, 36, 43]. More recent multimodal attacks jointly exploit both modalities, optimizing adversarial prompts together with image-level perturbations to evade multiple safety components within the same pipeline [8, 44], creating a substantially broader attack surface than any single-stage checker can cover. 

Diffusion RL. Diffusion RL aims to align generative models with downstream objectives that are difficult to optimize using standard supervised losses, including human preference, aesthetic quality, and prompt-image alignment. Existing methods typically formulate the denoising trajectory as a sequential decision process and compute rewards from the final generated image. DDPO [1] uses policy gradients with terminal image-level rewards, DPOK [10] adds KL regularization to constrain deviations from the pretrained model, and differentiable-reward methods such as AlignProp [34] and DRaFT [7] backpropagate rewards through the denoising trajectory. B 2-DiffuRL [18] further mitigates sparse credit assignment with backward progressive training. However, these methods still primarily rely on final image-level rewards, making it difficult to determine which intermediate denoising steps caused the desired or undesired outcome.

3.2 Diffusion RL Overview. We formulate the denoising process of a pretrained T2I model as a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP) [2, 4], in which the agent observes only the current denoising state rather than having access to the full denoising trajectory. And a lightweight actor-critic net-work intervenes at selected denoising timesteps to steer the generation trajectory away from unsafe content. Unlike prior diffusion RL approaches [1, 18] that fine-tune the entire denoising network end-to-end with terminal rewards, our framework keeps the pretrained model fully frozen and trains only the intervention policy with a decomposed reward combining timestep-weighted intermedi-ate NSFW feedback and a terminal aesthetic reward, providing dense credit assignment throughout the trajectory. Furthermore, unlike deterministic safe image generation methods that apply a fixed one-shot correction, our RL policy adaptively determines the direction and strength of intervention at each timestep, with each action reshaping the entire subsequent denoising trajectory. We fur-ther extend the framework to flow matching models, making our approach the first RL-based safety framework applicable to both diffusion and flow matching T2I models.

4 Experiments 

4.1 Datasets 

We construct our training dataset using prompts from the I2P dataset [38]. We collect 200 prompts per category (self-harm, violence, shocking content, hate, harassment, sexual, and illegal activity), resulting in 1,400 NSFW prompts in total. For the clean data, we use the COCO2014 captions dataset [24]. Each image is paired with five human-written captions: We take the first caption for each image and randomly sample 1,400 captions of these first captions. We construct a balanced training dataset (1,400 NSFW vs. 1,400 Clean prompts). For evaluation, we build a test dataset from the I2P dataset by sampling 100 prompts per category, ensuring no overlap with the training set. The only exception is the hate category, where only 47 remaining non-overlapping prompts are available. This results in 647 NSFW test prompts in total. To maintain a balance between NSFW and safe prompts, we sample 647 safe prompts from COCO2014 captions. We refer to this test set as Naive prompts, as it contains the original (non-attacked) prompts. To further assess robustness under adversarial attacks, we construct adversarial prompts by applying established prompt attack methods including P4D [6], MMA-Diffusion [44], SneakyPrompt [46], and Ring-A-Bell [43] to the Naive prompts. Evaluating on both Naive and Adversarial sets allows us to verify whether our approach remains effective even when prompts are attacked. 

4.2 Implementation Details 

We implement our method on Stable Diffusion v1.5 [37], Stable Diffusion v2.0, and Stable Diffusion v3.0 [9]. We opt for a DDIM Scheduler for SD v1.5 and SD v2.0 and a FlowMatch Euler scheduler for SD v3.0. For SD v1.5 and SD v2.0, the total number of denoising steps is T = 50 , and we apply the predicted-noise control agent every m = 5 steps, (i.e., at steps 45, 40, ..., 5). For SD v3.0, the total number of denoising steps is T = 28 , and we apply the control agent every m = 4 steps, i.e., at steps 24, 20, ..., 4. The shared encoder of the actor-critic network consists of a MLP having two hidden layers (256 hidden units each) with SiLU activation functions and LayerNorm, and a single-layer GRU with SiLU activation functions and 256-dimension hidden states. The complete hyperparameter settings and network architecture can be found in Appendix F. All experiments are conducted on a single NVIDIA A6000 GPU.

4.5 Robustness to In-Generation Attack 

While existing safe image generation methods operate at a single fixed stage of the pipeline and are therefore vulnerable to attacks injected during the denoising process, our framework continuously monitors the generation trajectory and can respond to unsafe signals at any timestep. To evaluate this capability further, we construct a mid-generation latent attack scenario that combines prompt sub-stitution with latent perturbation. Generation begins conditioned on a safe prompt; at a designated attack timestep t∗, the prompt is switched to a semantically matched NSFW prompt that preserves the overall scene context (e.g. subject, pose, composition), while replacing only the unsafe attribute (e.g. wearing clothes → nudity ). Simultaneously, an adversarial perturbation δadv is injected into the intermediate latent: 

zt∗ ← zt∗ + δadv , (31) where δadv is optimized via PGD [29] to maximize the NSFW score of the final generated image. We evaluate under three attack timesteps t∗ ∈ { 35 , 25 , 15 }, corresponding to early, mid, and late injection points in the denoising trajectory. Table 3 reports Inappropriate Probability against vanilla SD, SLD, and our method. Since the existing methods apply corrections at a fixed stage, they remain largely ineffective against mid-generation attacks. In contrast, our policy detects the injected per-turbation at the subsequent probe step and immediately corrects the trajectory, yielding consistently lower Inappropriate Probability across all attack timesteps. Full experimental details and results are provided in Appendix C.

C Details for Section 4.5 

We provide full experiment details for the robustness evaluation against mid-generation latent at-tacks described in Section 4.5. The goal of this experiment is to assess whether our framework can detect and correct NSFW content injected during the denoising process, a threat scenario that prompt-level and output-level defenses are structurally incapable of addressing. By injecting ad-versarial perturbations at intermediate timesteps and measuring the policy’s response, we directly validate the core claim that continuous trajectory monitoring enables timely in-generation safety enforcement. 

C.1 Dataset and Prompts. We construct the evaluation set using 200 prompts sampled from the sexual category of the I2P dataset [38], with no overlap with the training set. For each prompt, we pair it with a semantically matched safe prompt that preserves the same subject and scene com-position while removing unsafe attributes. Specifically, the paired safe prompt is constructed by replacing unsafe descriptors with neutral alternatives, while keeping the subject identity, pose, and scene context unchanged (e.g., wearing clothes → nudity ). Generation is initially conditioned on the safe prompt and the NSFW prompt is substituted at the attack timestep t∗, while the latent state 

zt∗ and random seed remain unchanged. This setting isolates the effect of mid-generation prompt substitution from other sources of variation. All experiments are conducted on SD v1.5 with 50 DDIM denoising steps and CFG scale ω = 7 .5.

C.2 Attack Setup. Generation is initially conditioned on a safe prompt and proceeds normally up to the attack timestep t∗. At t∗, two perturbations are applied simultaneously: (i) the conditioning prompt is switched from the safe prompt to a semantically matched NSFW prompt, and (ii) an adversarial perturbation δadv is injected directly into the intermediate latent: 

zt∗ ← zt∗ + δadv  (32) The perturbation δadv is optimized via projected gradient descent (PGD) [29] to maximize the NSFW score of the final decoded image, subject to an ℓ2 norm constraint ∥δadv ∥2 ≤ ρ. We set ρ = 0 .1

and run PGD for 20 steps with step size 0.01 . After injection, denoising resumes from zt∗ + δadv 

to z0 using the standard DDIM scheduler, conditioned on the substituted NSFW prompt. This combined attack bypasses prompt-level defenses entirely, as the initial prompt is safe, while the latent perturbation further amplifies the unsafe semantics introduced by the prompt switch. We evaluate under three attack timesteps t∗ ∈ { 35 , 25 , 15 }, corresponding to early-stage, mid-stage, and late-stage injection points respectively, using 200 prompts sampled from the I2P sexual category. 

C.3 Results. Table 14 compares Inappropriate Probability and CLIP Score across vanilla SD v1.5 [37], SLD [38], and our method under the three attack timesteps. Note that since prompt-level and output-level defenses including SAFREE [47], PurifyGen [3], UCE [11], and RECE [14] do not monitor or correct the intermediate denoising trajectory, they provide no meaningful protec-tion against latent-space injection attacks. SLD, while operating in latent space, applies a state-independent correction, thus remains vulnerable when the attack is injected after its correction has been applied. In contrast, our method continuously monitors the NSFW score ˆst and maintains the hidden state ht that accumulates evidence of trajectory deviation across timesteps. When an attack is injected, the resulting spike in ˆst is detected at the next probe step, and the policy immediately in-creases its intervention strength at to suppress the unsafe direction. This mechanism is particularly effective for early- and mid-stage attacks where sufficient denoising steps remain for the correction to propagate through the trajectory. F Implementation Details and the Hyperparameters 

Training Setup. All experiments are conducted on a single NVIDIA A6000 GPU. The pretrained T2I models — SD v1.5 [37], SD v2.0, and SD v3.0 [9] — are kept fully frozen throughout policy training. We use the DDIM scheduler [42] with T = 50 denoising steps for all diffusion based T2I models, and the FlowMatch Euler scheduler for flow matching T2I models. The policy is applied every 5 denoising steps for diffusion based T2I models and every 4 denoising steps for flow matching T2I model. A separate policy is trained for each T2I model with observation dim( ot) = 8 C + 2 ,where C = 4 is the number of channel for SD v1.5/v2.0 and C = 16 for SD v3.0. All policies are trained for 20,000 episodes with mini-batches of size 128 sampled from collected trajectories. We use the Adam optimizer with a fixed learning rate of 1 × 10 −4 and no learning rate scheduling. 

Policy Architecture. The actor-critic network consists of a two-layer MLP encoder ( (8 C + 2) →

256 , SiLU + LayerNorm) followed by a single-layer GRU with hidden size 256. The actor head is a linear projection from the GRU hidden state to two scalars (ut, v t) parameterizing the beta distribution. The critic head is a separate linear projection to a scalar value estimate. The total number of trainable parameters is approximately 0.8M for SD v1.5/v2.0 and 0.9M for SD v3.0. 

Latent Safety Probe. The latent safety probe gϕ is a lightweight convolutional network consist-ing of three convolutional blocks (each: Conv2d → BatchNorm → ReLU) with channel sizes 

(C, 32 , 64) , followed by global average pooling and a two-layer MLP with hidden size 128 and sigmoid output. The normalized timestep ˜t is concatenated to the MLP input. The probe contains approximately 0.4M parameters and is pretrained on Ddistill for 50 epochs with batch size 64 before policy training begins. The distillation dataset is collected from 5,000 frozen denoising trajectories (no policy intervention) using the training prompts described in Section 4.

G Limitations 

While our framework demonstrates strong empirical performance, several limitations remain. First, the latent safety probe gϕ is distilled from a fixed teacher pipeline (VAE decoding combined with external NSFW detectors), so its accuracy is fundamentally bounded by the coverage and biases of these teacher signals; unsafe concepts not well captured by the teacher detectors may not be reliably identified by the probe. Second, the action space is a single scalar intervention strength applied along one safe direction per timestep; this design may be insufficient for prompts that entangle multiple distinct unsafe concepts requiring different correction directions simultaneously. Third, our robustness evaluation focuses on PGD-based in-generation attacks under a specific threat model; robustness against other adaptive or black-box attack strategies not considered in this work remains an open question.
